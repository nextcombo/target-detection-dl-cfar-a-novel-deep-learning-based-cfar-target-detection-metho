# [Target Detection] DL-CFAR: A Novel Deep Learning-Based CFAR Target Detection Method [with Code]

Introduction: The core idea of the target detection scheme is to estimate the noise level in the RD (Range-Doppler) map around the test cell (CUT) based on the neighboring cells, and set appropriate thresholds to detect targets. The Cell-Average Constancy False Alarm Rate (CA-CFAR) [1] uses the arithmetic mean of reference cell power as an estimate of the noise level. Its variant, the Maximum Unit Constancy False Alarm Rate (GOCA-CFAR) [2] can improve the false alarm rate of the original scheme. Although these two schemes perform well in uniform scenarios, their performance degrades in multi-target scenarios based on error-doppler level estimation. A minimum cell average constancy false alarm rate (SOCA-CFAR) [3] is proposed to improve performance in multi-target scenarios. However, it does not significantly improve performance in dense multi-target scenarios. Ordered statistics constancy false alarm (OS-CFAR) [4] can handle such issues but introduces significant computational complexity. Therefore, it is necessary to study a multi-target robust scheme with lower computational complexity. This is the main motivation for this research.
Recently, the author [5] proposed using deep learning techniques to improve CACFAR detection performance while reducing false alarm rates. In [6], a Support Vector Machine model was proposed for selecting the best CFAR scheme under given conditions, introducing a reference window. However, these schemes only slightly outperform conventional schemes due to simply training models to simulate conventional schemes or select the best conventional schemes. We also observed that the degradation of the famous constancy false alarm plan occurs in high signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and multi-target scenarios. In such cases, strong sidelobes associated with target returns cause overestimated noise levels, leading to lower detection rates. Herein, for simplicity, this phenomenon is referred to as the sidelobe problem. If the sidelobes can be eliminated, then superior noise estimation results can be achieved.
In [7] and [8], the authors employ deep learning models to address denoising issues. Their work demonstrates that deep learning models can identify structured patterns and remove noise patterns in images. Building on this, this paper proposes a novel constant false alarm rate (CFAR) detection algorithm based on deep learning models, termed DL-CFAR. This model learns from the structure of targets in the RD graph and eliminates these structures to obtain an RD graph containing only noise, thereby enabling more accurate estimation of noise levels and achieving robust constant false alarm rate detection across various scenarios. This is the first attempt to directly enhance the process of constant false alarm rate noise estimation. The results show that the proposed scheme significantly outperforms traditional CFAR solutions. Moreover, the performance of conventional CFAR schemes is influenced by parameter settings (protection cell size, order selection). Inadequate parameter settings in traditional CFAR schemes can lead to significant system performance degradation. In contrast, DL-CFAR does not require parameter settings. Therefore, this method represents a practical approach for constant false alarm rate target detection.

Simulation Results and Discussion
In our simulations, we compared the proposed scheme with four well-known CFAR schemes: CA-CFAR, GOCA-CFAR, SOCA-CFAR, and OS-CFAR.
A. Training Signal-to-Noise Ratio
Note that in this document, SNR is defined as the SNR observed at the MRC RD bin. In the initial simulations, we determined the optimal training SNR for the proposed neural network. We trained multiple neural networks using different training datasets with varying signal-to-noise ratios. Subsequently, we tested these neural networks on a validation dataset including 21 data points with SNR values ranging from 19 to 39 dB, each of which has a size of 10,000. The table shows the validation loss results of the proposed neural networks under different SNRs. The neural network trained with an SNR of 28 dB performed best due to its lowest validation loss. Since learning the target structure in noisy environments is challenging, the performance of neural networks trained at lower signal-to-noise ratios slightly decreased. However, since neural networks trained in ideal (no noise) environments lack the ability to handle noise, the performance of neural networks trained at higher SNRs also slightly degraded. This indicates that the optimal training SNR is in the middle range value. Therefore, we employed an SNR of 28 dB for neural network training in the following simulations.
B. Noise Estimation Performance of DL-CFAR
Due to the importance of noise level estimation in constant false alarm rate (CFAR) schemes, we evaluated the test scheme from two aspects: deviation and standard deviation of noise level estimation. We evaluated OS-CFAR using different orders, but for simplicity, only the best two results are presented. Table II lists the deviation and standard deviation of noise level estimation for different CFAR schemes with different SNR and number of targets. In high signal-to-noise ratio or multi-target scenarios, due to sidelobe problems, CA-CFAR and its variants (SOCACFAR and GOCA-CFAR) have significantly increased deviation and standard deviation. Simulation results also indicate that although OS-CFAR has a lower standard deviation, it leads to more false alarms due to underestimation of noise level. The DL-CFAR algorithm has the best robustness under different signal-to-noise ratios and target counts because it solves the sidelobe problem by eliminating target patterns in the RD graph. Moreover, studies have shown that deep learning methods can adopt deeper architectures to automatically learn and find more suitable features, rather than using rule-based operations as other CFAR methods do.
In the simulations discussed below, we aim to discuss the trade-off between detection rate and false alarm rate in different CFAR schemes under a single target scenario. Based on experience, it is possible to successfully detect targets when the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (SNR) is greater than 30 to 35 dB [15]. Figure 3 shows the detection rates and false alarm rates of different CFAR schemes with low SNR. CA-CFAR and its variants perform well in homogeneous scenarios. DL-CFAR significantly outperforms other CFAR schemes. Figures 4 and 5 show the detection rates and false alarm rates of different CFAR schemes with medium and high SNR. As the test signal-to-noise ratio increases, the performance of all CFAR schemes improves due to the reduced difficulty in target detection. It is noteworthy that, under low false alarm rates, DL-CFAR significantly outperforms other schemes. This phenomenon indicates that even at extremely low false alarm rates, DL-CFAR can significantly enhance detection rates.
In summary, although other CFAR schemes use guard cells to handle sidelobe problems, their performance degrades because fixed-size guard cells cannot handle various types of sidelobes. The DL-CFAR algorithm employs neural network methods to identify and remove target patterns in different scenarios. The results show that DL-CFAR has superior performance compared to other CFAR schemes under different signal-to-noise ratio conditions.
In this section, we evaluated different CFAR schemes under multi-objective scenarios. Figure 6 illustrates the relationship between detection rate and false alarm rate for different CFAR schemes for a scenario with SNR = 20 dB and three targets. The performance of DL-CFAR outperforms other CFAR schemes. When the alarm rate is 10^-4, DL-CFAR achieves a detection rate improvement of about 6% over other CFAR schemes. Figure 7 presents simulation results for a four-target scenario with SNR = 20 dB. At a false alarm rate of 10^-4, DL-CFAR shows at least an 8% improvement in detection rate. In summary, all CFAR schemes show a significant decline in performance as the number of targets increases. However, when compared to DL-CFAR, which only slightly degrades in performance when the number of targets increases, the performance of traditional CFAR schemes drops significantly.
Although existing research has shown that some traditional constant false alarm rate (CFAR) schemes perform well in specific scenarios, such as uniform scenes where CA-CFAR outperforms other traditional CFAR schemes, these schemes generally perform poorly in multi-objective scenarios. In contrast, by adopting neural networks, DL-CFAR consistently achieves optimal performance in both single- and multi-objective scenarios. These results indicate that neural networks enable constant false alarm detection to adapt to various situations. The robustness of DL-CFAR against various environmental clutter warrants further investigation.
Conclusion This paper introduces for the first time a deep learning-based constant false alarm target detection algorithm. The DL-CFAR algorithm is a first attempt to improve the noise estimation process of CFAR detection through deep learning. Simulation results show that the DL-CFAR algorithm significantly outperforms classic CFAR algorithms in terms of performance under different signal-to-noise ratios and target counts. We hope this work encourages other researchers to incorporate advanced deep learning technologies into the field of target detection.
